Before a drive fails outright, it often spends weeks or months getting gradually worse: files that used to open instantly now take a second or two, programs are slower to launch, and the whole machine feels less responsive than it did. This is easy to write off as a computer just getting old.

Sometimes that is exactly what it is, and the cause is one of the other reasons covered in our guide to slow computers. But a gradual, ongoing slowdown with no other explanation is also the earliest sign a drive gives before the more serious signs below appear, which is why it is worth ruling out rather than living with.

- id: noises title: "Clicking, grinding or buzzing noises from inside the case" urgency: stop-now urgency_label: "Stop using it now" price_key: data-recovery-failing-drive text: |

A mechanical hard drive stores data on a spinning disk and reads it with an arm that physically moves into place above the surface. A healthy drive does this silently. A repeated clicking, grinding or buzzing sound means that arm is failing to find what it is looking for and is retrying, over and over, against a surface it may already be damaging further with each attempt.

This is the single clearest sign a drive gives, and it is also the most time-critical. Every retry is another chance for the read arm to scratch the disk surface itself, turning data that is still recoverable right now into data that is gone permanently. A drive making this noise should be shut down and left off until it is looked at.

- id: freezing title: "Random freezes, especially when opening files or programs" urgency: stop-now urgency_label: "Stop using it now" price_key: data-recovery-failing-drive text: |

A drive with damaged sectors will keep trying to read the same faulty spot before eventually giving up, and while it retries, the whole computer can lock up. It is a more serious sign than ordinary slowness, because everything dragging evenly is usually software or age, while a drive locking the whole machine up for several seconds at a time is the drive itself struggling to do its job.

It is easy to blame this on the specific program that froze, but the pattern, not the timing, is what matters. A drive doing this is actively struggling to read parts of itself, and continuing to use it in that state risks losing whatever is stored in the damaged sections before they can be recovered.

- id: corrupt-files title: "Files that will not open, or corrupt file errors that were not there before" urgency: stop-now urgency_label: "Stop using it now" price_key: data-recovery-failing-drive text: |

A document that opens as garbled text, a photo that will not display, or a straightforward "this file is corrupted" error on something that opened fine last week are all signs that the data itself is starting to degrade on the drive, not just that something is running slowly. This is different from a virus or a software fault, both of which usually leave the file itself intact.

The number of affected files tends to grow the longer the drive keeps running, because the same physical damage that corrupted the first file is still there and getting worse. Anything still opening normally right now is the best chance you will have to get it off the drive.

- id: boot-failure title: "The computer does not boot, or boots to a black screen some of the time" urgency: stop-now urgency_label: "Stop using it now" price_key: data-recovery-dead-drive text: |

Windows itself lives on the drive, so a drive in bad enough shape can fail to load the operating system at all. Early on this tends to be intermittent: the computer boots normally most of the time and occasionally hangs on a black screen or a spinning logo, then works again after a restart. Left alone, the intermittent failures usually become permanent.

A computer that will not boot at all, consistently, means the drive has likely gone from failing to failed. At that point nothing on the machine can be reached the normal way, and the files on it need to be recovered directly from the drive rather than through Windows. The sooner that happens after the drive stops working, the better the odds of getting everything back.
