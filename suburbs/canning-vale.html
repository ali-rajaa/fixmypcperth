Canning Vale is younger than it looks. Until the late 1970s it was market gardens and dairy farms, and most of the land now covered in housing was zoned rural until 1994. Then it grew fast: about 12,800 people in 2001, more than 23,000 by 2006, and roughly 34,500 by the 2021 census. Today it covers around 25 square kilometres, split between the City of Canning west of Nicholson Road and the City of Gosnells to the east.

That history changes what goes wrong. Newer homes tend to mean newer machines, so the dust-choked tower that has sat under a desk for a decade is less common here. What replaces it is everything a busy household connects at once. A single router struggles to reach every corner of a larger home, NBN plans promise speeds the setup never delivers, and gaming laptops and consoles run hot in bedrooms with little airflow.

Canning Vale also works for a living. North of the rail line sits one of Perth's big industrial areas, with warehousing and distribution centres and many smaller businesses alongside them. Plenty of them run on a handful of computers and nobody whose job it is to look after them.

Laptops in Canning Vale work harder than most. In one house there can be a school laptop, a uni laptop, a work-from-home laptop and a gaming laptop, and every one of them gets carried from room to room, into the car and back again. That shows up as physical wear: cracked screens, charging ports worked loose, hinges opened and closed a few thousand times too many.

It also shows up as heat. A gaming laptop packs a lot of power into a thin case, and after a year or two the thermal paste dries out and the vents clog. The fan gets loud, the keyboard gets hot, and performance falls away at exactly the moment it is needed. A repaste and a proper clean usually brings it back to how it ran new.

desktops_intro: |

Desktops in Canning Vale are more likely to be gaming rigs and home-office towers than a single family PC. The faults follow from that. A custom build throws a driver conflict, a new graphics card shows no picture, or the system crashes only when it is under load. An office tower slows down as software piles up and storage fills.

Most of that is fixable in an afternoon. A crash under load is often heat or power rather than a failed part, and a slow office machine usually needs storage and cleanup rather than replacing.

If the tower is awkward to move, on-site visits are available across the suburb, and anything that does not need hands on the hardware can often be handled over remote support.

Younger households keep a surprising amount of their life on one device: years of phone photos synced to a single laptop, school work, tax records, a small business's invoices. When that device fails, it rarely gives much warning.

For the businesses in the industrial area the stakes are higher still. A dead drive can take quotes, job records and accounts with it, and cost a day or more of work that simply cannot happen. The cheapest recovery is the one you never need: a backup that runs by itself, to the cloud or to an external drive, set up once and then left alone.
