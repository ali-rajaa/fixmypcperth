why_local: |

Thornlie was built in stages over more than sixty years, and each stage left a different kind of household behind. The north-east of the suburb went up in the 1950s and 60s. South Thornlie and Crestwood followed in the 1970s, Castle Glen in the 1980s, and Forest Lakes has kept growing from the 1980s to the present. With about 23,600 people across roughly 12 square kilometres, it is the largest suburb in the City of Gosnells.

That spread matters more than any average. On one street the computer is a recent family laptop. A few streets over it is a desktop that has run a household's email and banking for years and has never once been opened up. The population has held steady since the 1990s, so a lot of people have been in the same home, and on the same computer, for a long time.

Machines that have been in service that long rarely fail all at once. They fail through a slow build-up: an operating system that no longer gets security updates, a printer that stops talking to it after a router change, an email account nobody can get back into once the password is forgotten.

Across much of Thornlie the laptop is the household's main computer rather than one of several. It is where the email lives, where the banking gets done, and where the photos end up. So when it goes wrong, it takes everything with it.

The faults that turn up on laptops like that are the ones time brings: a battery that no longer lasts an hour off the charger, a keyboard with a few dead keys, a screen that flickers when the lid moves. None of those mean the laptop is finished. A battery or keyboard is a routine replacement, and a flicker is often a loose cable rather than a failed panel.

In the newer streets of Forest Lakes the pattern looks more like any young family suburb: school laptops, cracked screens and chargers that have been stood on.

desktops_intro: |

Thornlie still has plenty of desktop computers, often a tower bought for the household years ago and never replaced because it never quite stopped working. Those machines are usually worth keeping. They were built to be opened, the parts are easy to get, and a couple of targeted upgrades can make an old tower feel far quicker.

The first question with any older desktop is whether it can run a supported version of Windows. Some towers can move to Windows 11 as they are. Others fall short of Microsoft's hardware requirements, usually because of the processor, and for those the honest answer may be that replacing it makes more sense than upgrading it.

If carrying the tower out to the car is the hard part, we can come to you instead.

data_intro: |

When a computer has been in the same home for many years, the only copy of a great deal tends to live on it: decades of photos carried across from older machines, letters, tax records, the family history someone spent years putting together. None of it was meant to depend on a single drive, but it usually does.

Older drives often give some warning before they go. Clicking or grinding sounds, files that take far longer to open than they used to, and error messages about the disk are all worth acting on early rather than waiting to see what happens next.
