When a computer stops working, the files on it usually have not gone anywhere. The drive that holds them is often still readable even when the laptop will not turn on, Windows will not start, or the computer has been dropped. Getting those files back is a different job from fixing the computer, and it is often the part that matters most.

The one thing that makes recovery harder is time. A failing drive gets worse every time it is powered on, and every restart, every recovery program downloaded off the internet and every "let me just try one more thing" can destroy files that were still readable an hour before. If a drive is clicking, freezing or disappearing, turn the computer off and bring it in.

Hard drive recovery is a fixed price, one for a drive that is failing and one for a drive that has stopped altogether, and you pay nothing if we cannot recover your files. External drives, USB sticks and memory cards have their own lower price.

A hard drive that is starting to fail rarely stops all at once. It develops areas it can no longer read, and it keeps retrying them, which is why the computer freezes, slows down and throws errors. Mechanical drives often make noise as they go. The drive is still readable at this stage, but each hour of use damages it further.

We take the drive out and copy everything readable off it in a controlled way, carefully working around the damaged areas instead of hammering them the way Windows does. Then we check the recovered files open, and copy them to a new drive or a drive you bring. If we cannot recover your files, you pay nothing.

Our guide to the signs a hard drive is failing explains how urgent each warning sign is.

A drive that will not spin, will not mount or has disappeared from the computer needs more work than a failing one, and it is the advanced recovery price. It is still often recoverable: the fault may be in the drive's electronics or its firmware rather than the disks that hold your files.

We assess the drive first and tell you honestly how likely recovery is before starting. If we cannot recover your files, you pay nothing. Do not open the drive, freeze it or tap it, whatever the internet suggests, since each of those can turn a recoverable drive into one that is not.

- id: ssd group: "Hard drive and SSD recovery" title: "SSD data recovery" price_key: data-recovery-failing-drive also_keys: - data-recovery-dead-drive signs: - "SSD suddenly missing from the computer" - "Drive shows the wrong size or as read-only" - "Computer freezes on the same files every time" - "Windows will not start after a crash" text: |

SSDs fail differently from mechanical drives. There are no moving parts and no warning noises, and when an SSD goes, it can go suddenly and completely. Some fail into a read-only state, which is the good outcome, since everything on them can still be copied off.

Many SSDs also clear deleted data automatically in the background, so the sooner a problem SSD is turned off and brought in, the better the odds. An SSD that is failing but readable is the failing-drive price. One that has stopped responding is the dead-drive price.

- id: laptop-wont-start group: "Hard drive and SSD recovery" title: "Files from a laptop or PC that will not start" price_key: data-recovery-failing-drive signs: - "Laptop dead after a drop or spill" - "Windows will not start" - "Screen broken, so you cannot see anything" - "Old computer you want the photos off" text: |

The computer not working does not usually mean the drive has failed. A dead motherboard, a broken screen or a Windows that will not boot all leave the files where they were. We take the drive out and recover what is on it, whether or not the computer itself is worth fixing.

If the drive is healthy and only the computer has failed, it is often a straightforward copy, and we tell you when that is the case. If the computer is worth repairing, the laptop repair page covers that side.

- id: deleted-files group: "Hard drive and SSD recovery" title: "Deleted files and formatted drives" price_key: data-recovery-failing-drive signs: - "Emptied the recycle bin by mistake" - "Formatted or reset the wrong drive" - "Files gone after a Windows reinstall" - "A folder of photos deleted months ago" text: |

Deleting a file removes the record of where it is, not the file itself, at least until something new is saved over it. On a mechanical hard drive, that gives a real chance of getting it back. On an SSD the odds are lower and time matters more.

Stop using the drive straight away, and do not install recovery software onto it, since that can overwrite the very files you want back. We quote it before we start, based on the drive and what happened.

  • Turn the computer or drive off. Every hour it runs can damage a failing drive further.
